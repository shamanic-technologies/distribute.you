What an LLM judge is

Jev is a model by TypeSafe that answers questions instead of writing text. You send a piece of text and a typed question: yes or no, one choice among options, or a level on a scale. Jev sends back a probability for every answer.

The probability is the point. A writing model such as DeepSeek V4.1 Flash or Gemini 3 gives you one answer, said with the same confidence whether it is sure or guessing. Jev tells you when it is guessing.

Jev 1.13 bills input tokens only. The answer is free. Across our pipes, 1,000 judgments come to {{raw volume.perThousand}} as billed.

How to use an LLM judge for GTM

Four rules cover most of what we learned.

  • Every "is this an X?" is a judgment. Is this lead in my audience? Is this reply from a person? Is this sender a real buyer? A keyword rule answers these badly and in one language. A judge reads the text the way you would.
  • Ask before you pay. Put the judgment in front of the expensive step, never after it.
  • Judge once, keep the verdict. Store the answer with the exact question and text it was given. Ask again only when one of them changes.
  • Read the probability, set the bar per decision. Being wrong costs more in some places than others, so the bar moves with the stake.

Case 2: is this reply from a person?

A reply rate looks great until you read the replies.

{{chart replies}}

{{raw machine.display}} of the replies to our cold emails are written by a machine: out-of-office messages and other auto-replies. A dashboard that counts them all counts the machines too, and every decision built on it inherits the error.

The probability helps on the hard ones. A clinic CEO wrote "Unfortunately I can't till next week." One reading says out of office, another says a meeting request. Jev split its answer across three readings and gave a confidence of 22%. That is Jev saying "a person should read this". So a person does.

On cold email replies, Jev runs beside our reply classifier, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and checks its work. On the inbox it does decide: a sender judged automated is hidden from your contacts only when Jev is at least 75% sure.

Case 3: the bar we set too high

The lead screen started with a bar of 80%: a person had to be very likely to fit. Real buyers sat between 50% and 80%, so the bar was turning them away. We lowered it to 50% on September 29.

The fix was easy because Jev is sure most of the time. {{raw screen.sure}} of its answers on the screen are under 20% or at 80% and up. The bar only matters in the middle, and you can read that middle before you pick it.

Our bars today: 50% to screen a lead, because losing a real buyer hurts more than one extra reveal. 75% before we hide a sender from your contacts, because hiding a real person hurts more than showing one robot.

Judge once, keep the verdict

Every verdict is stored with the audience, the person, the exact audience text and the version of the question. The same person met twice is not judged twice. When a client rewrites their audience, the text changes, so everyone is judged again against the new words.

The stored verdict is also the record. When a client asks why someone was skipped, the answer is the question, the text and the probability, kept as they were.
