Key takeaway

On average across our clients, the same budget reaches 4x more leads that match their ICP.

  • You write your ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) in one sentence
  • Jev checks each Apollo lead against it, and against the signals you pick, before we pay to reveal the email
  • For one client, Jev checked 3,610 people from Apollo; 107 matched the ICP

Apollo filters were built for job titles and company sizes. An ICP is usually a sentence. Here is what we saw in our own data when we let an AI read that sentence for each lead.

One client, one sentence

One of our clients is a medical-device company selling shockwave devices to chiropractors. Their ICP: owners of chiropractic clinics in the United States. We set the Apollo filters that come closest (title, industry, country, size) and Apollo returned a long list.

Jev checked 3,610 of those people against the ICP. 107 matched. A "Chiropractic Physician-Owner" was a clear yes. A "Hospital Medicine Physician", whom the filters had let in, was a clear no: skipped before the paid reveal, so no credit was spent on that lead.

The filters were not wrong to return those people. Titles and industries come close to an ICP; they are not the same thing. The gap between the two is where a cold email budget can quietly leak.

What Jev does

Jev 1.13 is a model by TypeSafe built to judge rather than to write. You give it a piece of text and a question; it answers yes or no and says how sure it is. We placed it between Apollo and the paid reveal. Across our pipes it answered 120k+ questions since September 19, 2026.

  1. You write your ICP in one sentence. In your words, not in dropdown values.
  2. Jev checks each Apollo lead against it. The name, title and company are free to read; the email costs a credit, so the check comes first.
  3. You add the signals that make a lead hot. Our AI suggests some for your offer, you pick the ones you want, and Jev reads the evidence on the company website, its job posts or its LinkedIn page. Real examples from our clients: "Does the company website mention an FDA 510(k) clearance?", "Does the company have open job postings for QA engineers?", and for the client above: "Does the clinic's website show no sign that it belongs to a national shockwave clinic network?"

What it changes, on average

Across our clients, the same budget reaches 4x more leads that match their ICP. The reveal is paid for the leads Jev keeps, and the check itself costs a fraction of a cent per lead. The ratio depends on how close the filters already are to the ICP: for the client above, it was far higher.

The questions we get

"What if Jev is wrong?" It can be, so the rule leans toward keeping a lead: anyone Jev finds more likely in the ICP than not is kept. We started stricter and lowered the bar on September 29 when we saw good leads just below it.

"Why not tighter Apollo filters?" We use them too, and they come first. But some ICPs are hard to filter: "independent clinic, not part of a national network" lives on the clinic's website, not in a dropdown.

"Does the check cost extra?" A fraction of a cent per lead, against about $0.10 for a reveal. Each verdict is stored, so the same lead is not checked twice.

"What if my ICP changes?" Rewrite the sentence. The leads are checked again against the new words.

Replies, too

The same idea runs on replies. More than half of the replies to our cold emails are out-of-office messages or auto-replies, in many languages ("Ich geniesse gerade Ferien"). Our reply classifier, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, labels them and Jev double-checks the label, so the replies you read and the numbers you track come mostly from real people.
