We bring our clients signups and booked meetings, done for you, and they report a 2.0x median ROI. Part of the job is picking who gets an email. A founder knows a good lead when they see the lead's website. So we let an AI look at the website too, and answer the founder's own yes/no questions about it. One client: roofers with no phone number in the header

One of our clients sells to roofing companies. A roofer whose phone number is missing from the top of the website loses calls, and that roofer is this client's lead. So the client's question for each prospect reads, word for word:

"Is there no clearly visible phone number in the header of the homepage above the fold?"

Until October 21, 2026, our AI answered in two steps. First, a model wrote a short description of the homepage screenshot. Then a judge, Jev 1.13, answered the question on that description. Here is a real description of one roofer's homepage, as stored in our database:

"The homepage features the main headline: "[headline]." The company offers residential and commercial roofing services in [their area], including replacement, repair, and inspection. There are two visible call-to-action buttons [...]. The page design is clean, modern, and highly readable."

The header of that page shows a phone number. The description does not mention one. Asked "is there no phone number?" on this text, the judge said yes.

Now the judge reads the screenshot itself and answers the client's question on it. When a description is enough

The two ways part only on what the description leaves out. We also re-judged two questions a page description is written to cover, from two other clients: "is a clear call to action missing above the fold?" and "does the design look outdated?". On those, the two ways gave the same answer on all but one of {{n coveredTotals.decided}} pages, and that one was a matter of taste. So asking on the image costs nothing in accuracy where the description was fine, and fixes the questions where the description was blind.

The rule

  1. Write each signal as a yes/no question, in your words: "Is there no phone number in the header?", "Does the site show a review rating?".
  2. Take a screenshot of the prospect's homepage, first screen.
  3. Ask the question on the screenshot. A judge reads the image and answers yes or no with how sure it is. No summary in between.

The judge sees what your eye sees, so a lead is kept or skipped for what is on the page.

The questions we get

"Is asking on the image expensive?" No. One check on a screenshot is a small fraction of a cent, and each answer is stored, so the same page is not judged twice for the same question.

"What if a cookie banner covers the page?" The judge first answers whether the screenshot really shows the homepage. A page hidden behind a banner, or an error page, is marked unreadable and not counted as a yes.

"Which AI should read the image?" On the phone question, {{n judges.count}} judges from three vendors each answered at least {{n judges.min}} of {{n judges.homepages}} right when they read the screenshot. Judges differ more on harder questions, so test yours on pages you checked by eye.

"Can I ask about anything on the page?" About anything visible on the first screen. A question about text deeper in the site reads the page text instead.
