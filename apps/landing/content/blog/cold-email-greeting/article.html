Key takeaway

The cold email greeting that gets the click is not the one that gets the reply:

  • $2 per website visit with no greeting, against $4 with the first name alone and $4 with "Hi" and the first name
  • $145 per positive reply with "Hi" and the first name, against $205 with the first name alone and $223 with no greeting

The question

Every cold email opens somehow. "Hi Marie," is the polite default. "Marie," alone is shorter and reads like a note from a colleague. Some emails skip both and open on the point.

We write cold emails for our clients, and until recently the model writing them picked the opening on its own. So all three openings are in our data. We counted which one costs less for the two things a first email can ask for: a click, or a reply.

The data

We looked at 109,000 cold emails from two kinds of campaign. One asks the prospect to visit a website and carries a link: 16,000 emails. The other asks for a positive reply, a prospect answering with interest: 93,000 emails. Auto-replies, out-of-office messages and refusals do not count as positive replies.

Each email is filed under how the first email of its sequence opened, so a follow-up's result counts for the opening the prospect saw first.

The answer

The greeting that gets the click is not the one that gets the reply. When the email asks for a website visit, skipping the greeting was cheapest: a visit cost you $2 with no greeting, against $4 with the first name alone.

When the email asks for a reply, the order flips. "Hi" plus the first name was cheapest: a positive reply cost you $145, against $223 with no greeting.

The rule this points to

An email that asks for a click can skip the hello. An email that asks for a conversation says hello first.

Pick the opening by what the email asks for, not by habit. The same "Hi Marie," that earns the cheapest reply was not the cheapest way to earn a visit.

Why the two outcomes disagree

Our reading, not something this data proves on its own. A website visit is a one-click decision. An email that gets straight to the point puts the offer and the link in front of the reader sooner, and a salutation adds nothing to that decision. A reply is the start of a conversation, and people answer people. "Hi Marie," reads as one person writing to another, which is the tone a prospect answers.

What this data cannot tell you

  • This is not a split test: the three openings ran for different clients, audiences and months. Read it as a direction.
  • Positive replies are rare. With no greeting there are only 3, so the reply ranking is directional.
  • The visit result depends on the model tier. With our Flash models, no greeting was cheapest. With our Pro models, "Hi" and the first name was cheapest at $3, but on only 7 visits, too few to call.
  • Our templates have required a greeting since September 28, 2026. Every email here was written before that.
  • 4 of the positive replies were recorded by the client in their CRM (a phone call, a LinkedIn message).

Method

Every cold email sent from April 15, 2026, read from our production database on September 29, 2026. Only people we started writing to at least 21 days before the read count, with every outcome they sent since. A website visit is counted only on emails that carry a link. The opening is read from the first email's text by a regex, no model involved: a greeting word in any language we write in (Hi, Hallo, Bonjour) counts as a greeting; a first name followed by a comma, a colon or a dash counts as the first name alone; anything else is no greeting. 6 emails whose first email text is not on record are left out.
