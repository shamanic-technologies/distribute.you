Key takeaway

A cold email that does not name the client gets more positive replies, and cheaper ones:

  • 0.05% positive reply rate without the name, against 0.02% with it
  • $190 per positive reply without the name, against $272 with it

The question

A cold email that promotes a company can name it: the company name, a link to its website, a direct recommendation. Or it can hold the name back: no name, no link, and an offer to introduce the prospect to the team behind it.

Naming reads as transparent. Holding back reads as a teaser. We write cold emails for our clients and have sent both kinds, so we counted which one gets more prospects to answer with interest, and which one costs less per interested answer.

The data

We looked at 94,000 cold emails whose only goal was a positive reply: a prospect answering with interest. Auto-replies, out-of-office messages and refusals do not count.

Each email sits on a side by what its prompt told the writing model to do, read from the prompt itself, never from the template's name. One side names the client. The other keeps the name and the link out and offers an introduction instead.

The answer

Client not named wins, on rate and on cost. 0.05% of the emails that kept the name back got a positive reply, against 0.02% of the emails that named the client.

A positive reply cost you $190 when the client was not named, against $272 when it was.

The rule this points to

When the goal is a reply, keep the client's name and link out of the first email and offer the introduction. The name can come once the prospect has answered and asked to hear more.

This is about emails that ask for a reply. An email meant to bring someone to a website needs the name and the link, so it is not part of this comparison.

Why holding the name back can earn more replies

Our reading, not something this data proves on its own. A named company with a link reads like an ad, and the prospect can judge it in one click without writing back. An email that holds the name back leaves one way to learn more: reply. It also asks for less, a short introduction rather than a purchase.

What this data cannot tell you

  • This is not a split test. The two sides ran for different clients, audiences and months. Read it as a direction, not as the measured effect of the name alone.
  • Positive replies are rare: 26 without the name, 9 with it. The gap in rate is unlikely to be chance (two-proportion test, p = 0.04).
  • The gap in cost is not settled (p = 0.47). An email without the name cost you $0.09 to write and send, against $0.06 with it, which narrows the price difference.

Method

Every cold email sent from 15 April 2026 by campaigns whose goal was a positive reply, read from our production database on 28 September 2026. Emails sent in the last 12 days before the read are left out, since 95% of positive replies arrive within that time. Client not named: 53,625 emails, 26 positive replies, $4,939 spent, 18 template versions. Client named: 40,681 emails, 9 positive replies, $2,451 spent, 16 template versions. 6 emails whose template was not recorded are left out. The cost is what the emails cost you to write and send.
