The bet

Every AI vendor now sells the same model family in tiers. Flash, the cheapest. Pro, the middle. Frontier, the most expensive. Pro costs about 5x Flash per token, and everyone has a reflex about it: the pricier model must convert better, so the question is whether it converts enough better to pay for itself.

The test

We wrote 125,000 cold emails with both tiers, on real campaigns for 34 clients, and counted two things per dollar spent: how many people clicked the link, and how many wrote back with interest. Not one prompt per tier: our campaigns run a catalogue of writing workflows, each for its own clients. That is not a split test, so every finding below says whether it still holds when we compare the two tiers on the same clients in the same months (a conclusion), only on the raw counts (a signal), or not at all (noise).

The answer

Flash buys a website visit cheaper than Pro. The Flash tier pays $2 per visit, Pro $5, and the gap holds when we compare the two tiers on the same clients in the same months: a conclusion. On positive replies, nothing is settled. The Pro tier pays $164; Flash pays $1,265, on a single reply, far too few to compare: noise.

23 workflows, and why we crown none

A workflow is one prompt on one model. We ran 23 of them past a thousand emails each. The cheapest of 23 always looks better than it is: part of its lead is luck, and each ran for different clients. On positive replies the cheapest workflows rest on one or two replies each, and on website visits the gap between the cheapest ones does not hold on the same clients. So we name no winning workflow: noise.

Every campaign still runs on the workflow with the best measured cost for what it buys, and that pick moves as the numbers move. What we can state is the tier result above.

Links and replies: close, not settled

Pro emails with no link got 5.6 positive replies per 10,000, against 1.3 with a link. Our reading is that a link turns a reply into a click. But the gap rests on a handful of replies and does not clear chance, even on the same clients in the same months: noise for now, and we will say so when it settles.

The same ten cuts, Flash against Pro

We cut the click article ten ways. Here is the same knife on the tier question: for each cut, what a website visit costs on Flash and on Pro, on the emails that carried a link, and where Pro earns its positive replies. Every bucket with an outcome is drawn, with its counts under the bar. A thin bucket is a thin read and is labelled as one. None of these cuts is checked on the same clients in the same months, and the positive-reply bars rest on a few replies each: read them as signals at best, never as conclusions.

1. Which email in the sequence

The first email does the work on both tiers. Flash buys the first-email visit for $1, Pro for $3. For the reply, the first two emails are where Pro earns it: 5.7 and 5.3 positive replies per 10,000 against 1.5 for the second follow-up.

2. Length of the email

Short wins the visit on Flash: under 400 characters costs $1 against $2 from 500 characters up. Pro loses the visit past 800 characters, at $7. The reply reads the other way: Pro's best reply band is 650 to 800 characters, at 13.1 per 10,000, a length Flash almost never writes.

3. Paragraphs

One paragraph buys the Flash visit for $1 and the Pro one for $3; six or more cost Pro $7. The reply wants the room: four or five paragraphs is Pro's best reply band, at 7.8 positive replies per 10,000.

4. Subject length

A subject of 30 characters or fewer buys the Flash visit for $1; past 40 characters it costs $3. Pro is $3 to $4 across every band it can price. The reply does not care much either way: Pro's replies are spread evenly from 21 characters up.

5. Seniority of the reader

A director clicks a Flash email for $1 and a Pro one for $2. Founders and owners barely click at all. They reply: owners lead Pro at 8.1 positive replies per 10,000 and founders follow at 5, against 1.9 for a director.

6. Size of the company

Big companies click cheap: over 1,000 people, a Flash visit is $1. The one to ten person company is the dearest Flash visit, $7, and Pro's best reply rate, 6.6 positive replies per 10,000 against 2.3 one size band up.

7. Industry

IT and services click for $2 on Flash and $7 on Pro. They barely write back at all, 1.3 positive replies per 10,000. Health and wellness practices do the opposite, 9.2 per 10,000, behind management consulting and financial services.

8. Where the reader is

Europe clicks cheaper than North America on both tiers: $1 against $2 on Flash, $3 against $6 on Pro. The replies are North American, because that is where the reply-seeking campaigns ran.

9. Local time of delivery

Flash clicks cheapest late, $1 between 8pm and midnight in the reader's own time zone, and dearest in the 6am to 8am rush at $3. Pro is cheapest in the small hours at $3. The reply likes the morning: 6am to 8am and 9am to 11am are the two best windows of the day, at 7.3 and 7.1 positive replies per 10,000.

10. Day of the week

Sunday is the cheapest Flash visit at $1 and Thursday the dearest at $3; on Pro, Monday is the cheapest day at $4. Monday carries the working week, at 6.2 positive replies per 10,000; the only day above it is Saturday, at 6.6.

The rule

Why

A visit is cheap to earn: a decent email with a link, and some people click. A reply is expensive to earn: someone has to read, believe and type. Across all 125,000 emails a positive reply was far rarer than a click. Whether the stronger model earns its keep on the reply is the question we cannot answer yet: Flash has too few positive replies to compare.

We have not tested the Frontier tier yet. At 5 to 10x the Pro price, the reply would have to get a lot cheaper still. We will publish that when we have it.

Method

  • Scope: sales cold email outreach only. Journalist outreach, PR pitches and every other channel we run are excluded.
  • Window: 15 April to 11 September 2026, every client account. Production data from the distribute.you platform, read on 2 October 2026, counting only the clicks and replies that had happened by 12 September 2026.
  • Emails too young to count: an email sent today has not had time to earn its click or its reply, so counting it makes every price read too high. We measured how long an outcome takes to arrive after the email that earned it: 95 in 100 website visits within 11 days, 95 in 100 positive replies within 9.6 days, measured on emails at least 45 days old so a slow one had time to show. The emails sent in the last 11 days of the window, from 1 September 2026 on (13,915 of them), are left out of every figure on this page, their clicks and replies with them.
  • Volume: 125,117 emails sent to 37,636 people across 36 clients. Every price and rate on this page is read on the 111,202 old enough to count, to 33,411 people across 34 clients: $6,683 of platform spend, 691 website visits and 34 positive replies. Of those emails, 38,785 carried a link to the client's site; they cost $2,104 and 623 people clicked. 51 workflows ran with tracked spend (a workflow is one prompt and one model), 23 of them past 1,000 emails.
  • Tiers: Flash, the cheapest model of a family (Gemini Flash, Claude Haiku, DeepSeek V4 Flash, GLM Flash, Kimi K2.6). Pro, the middle (Gemini Pro, Claude Opus, DeepSeek V4 Pro, GLM 5.3, Kimi K3). Frontier, the most expensive (Claude Fable, Astra), not yet run in production beyond a handful of emails. The model is read per email from the generation record.
  • Spend: everything the workflow cost before per-account discounts: lead sourcing, enrichment, sending and tokens. Not tokens alone. Each email carries its own workflow's cost per email, so a bucket's spend is the sum over the emails in it.
  • Pricing: every dollar here is what the client is charged, all in: lead sourcing, email verification, one email written for each recipient, the mailboxes and domains, sending, follow-ups, the team running it and our margin. There is no seat and no retainer on top.
  • Website visit: the sending platform reported that the recipient clicked a link in the email, attributed to the email the platform named. Counted once per person, on the first click. Emails with no link in the body are excluded from every visit price, because a click needs a link to land on.
  • Link scanners: a corporate mail gateway fetches every URL in a message before the reader sees it, so a raw hit on a tracking link is not a person. On the emails we send ourselves, a hit is classified before it counts: a HEAD request, a known scanner user agent, a browser string carrying a full build number that no current browser reports, or the same reader fetching the unsubscribe link within a minute. There is no time threshold, because a person on a phone reads a message within seconds too. 372 of 411 such hits were demoted. Clicks the sending provider reports on its own infrastructure are not affected, and they are most of this window: 690 of the 691 clicks counted here come from its tracking, 1 from our own redirect.
  • Positive reply: a recipient wrote back with interest (a question, a request for details, a meeting). Classified automatically, reviewed by a person on the account. Counted once per person, on the last email sent at or before the reply. Negative replies, out-of-office and unsubscribes do not count. Measured on every email, since a reply needs no link.
  • Cost per outcome: spend divided by outcomes. Every bucket with at least one outcome is drawn and priced, and its emails and outcomes are printed under the bar so a reader can weigh it. A bucket under 100 emails, or with no outcome at all, is a thin read and is labelled (thin): the send volume is what makes a bucket unreadable, and a few hundred emails is a real send. Crowning a workflow is the stricter question, and keeps its own bar: 1,000 emails and 10 positive replies. We name no winning workflow: picked out of many, with each run for different clients, the cheapest one's lead is partly luck.
  • Length, paragraphs, subject, links: measured on the generated text of the email that was sent, for each step of the sequence. A link is a URL or a bare domain in that text; the sending layer turns both into a tracked link.
  • Reader: country, seniority, company size and industry from the lead enrichment record. Local time from the reader's time zone where known.
  • Other studies: quoted as published, read on the date beside each. We compute nothing from them.
  • Reproducing this: the queries and the derivation are public on GitHub. Every figure on this page comes out of them.
  • Limits: not a randomised experiment. Our optimiser assigns workflows by measured cost, so the tier, the prompt, the audience and the ask are correlated: the reply-seeking campaigns ran on Pro, in North America, without a link. Each cut is reported alone. 34 positive replies is a small count. We report counts and no confidence intervals.
