The bet

Every AI vendor now sells the same model family in tiers. Flash, the cheapest. Pro, the middle. Frontier, the most expensive. Pro costs about 5x Flash per token, and everyone has a reflex about it: the pricier model must convert better, so the question is whether it converts enough better to pay for itself.

The test

We wrote 117,000 cold emails with both tiers, on real campaigns for 32 clients, and counted two things per dollar spent: how many people clicked the link, and how many wrote back with interest. Not one prompt per tier. Every client runs an A/B test across our whole catalogue of writing workflows, so the question we can answer is sharper than Flash or Pro: which workflow, on which tier, buys the outcome for the least, and by how much it beats the average.

The answer

Our best Pro workflow buys a positive reply for $122. The Pro tier as a whole pays $193; Flash got one reply in 34,305 emails. Our best Flash workflow buys a website visit for $1. The Flash tier as a whole pays $2, Pro $4. Each tier wins one match, and inside each tier the winning workflow beats its own average.

The A/B test: 17 workflows, one winner per outcome

A workflow is one prompt on one model, and on this page a workflow is a number, not a name. We ran 17 of them past a thousand emails each, 13 on Pro and 4 on Flash, on the same kinds of lists. The spread between them is the whole point of running the test. The best Pro workflow turned 7.6 emails in 10,000 into a positive reply; the Pro tier averaged 4.2; 4 Pro workflows got none at all, and no other workflow has enough replies to chart against it yet. On the click, where every workflow has a price, the Pro tier runs from $1 a visit to $37.

This is the gap distribute.you gives its clients. Every campaign runs on the workflow with the best measured cost for what that campaign buys, across every client. A client never gets the average of the test. They get its winner, and the winner changes when the numbers do.

The twist: the email decides the outcome, not the model

Of the 36 positive replies, every one came from an email with no link in it. The website visits came from the 21,189 emails that carried a link to the client's site, and from nowhere else. An email that asks for a click gets clicks. An email that asks for a reply gets replies. The tier only changes the price of what the email asked for: on the reply, Pro wins by a factor of 7; on the click, Flash wins by 2.

Every visit price in this article is therefore measured on the emails that carried a link. An email with nothing to click cannot buy a website visit, whatever wrote it.

The same ten cuts, Flash against Pro

We cut the click article ten ways. Here is the same knife on the tier question: for each cut, what a website visit costs on Flash and on Pro, on the emails that carried a link, and where Pro earns its positive replies. Every bucket is drawn, with its counts under the bar. A thin bucket is a thin read.

1. Which email in the sequence

The first email does the work on both tiers. Flash buys the first-email visit for $2, Pro for $5; on the second email the two tiers meet at $2. For the reply, the first two emails carry 30 of Pro's 35, at $149 and $174; the third email pays $396.

2. Length of the email

Short wins the click on both tiers: 300 to 499 characters costs $1 on Flash and $4 on Pro, and Pro's emails past 700 characters bought 6 visits in 2,314 emails. The reply reads the other way: Pro's replies come from 300 to 999 characters, and the 700 to 999 band, which only Pro writes, replies most often at 9.1 per 10,000.

3. Paragraphs

One or two paragraphs buy the visit for $1 on Flash and $3 on Pro; five or six cost $6 and $8. The reply wants the room: five or six paragraphs is Pro's best reply band at $119, one or two paragraphs got 4 replies in 21,448 emails.

4. Subject length

A 36 to 50 character subject buys the Flash visit for $1; on Pro the sweet spot is 21 to 35 at $4. The reply agrees with Pro: 21 to 35 characters carries 20 of its 35 replies at $160.

5. Seniority of the reader

C-suite readers click a Flash email for $1 and a Pro one for $3. Founders and owners barely click at all, on either tier. They reply: owners are Pro's cheapest reply at $108, founders next at $146, and the C-suite, which clicked for $1, replied once in 8,701 Pro emails.

6. Size of the company

Big companies click cheap: over 1,000 people, a visit is $1 on either tier. The one to ten person company is the dearest click on both ($7 Flash, $12 Pro) and Pro's cheapest reply at $135: 24 of its 35 replies came from there.

7. Industry

IT and services click for $2 on Flash and $4 on Pro, hospitals for $1 and $3. Health and wellness practices do not click, they write back: 13 positive replies at $96 each, Pro's best industry for the reply.

8. Where the reader is

Europe clicks cheaper than North America on both tiers: $1 against $2 on Flash, $3 against $5 on Pro. The replies are North American: 28 of Pro's 35, at $195, because that is where the reply-seeking campaigns ran.

9. Local time of delivery

The 9 to 12 window is the cheapest click on both tiers: $1 on Flash, $2 on Pro. Pro's evening emails pay $20 a visit. The reply likes the early morning: 6 to 9 local carries 19 of Pro's 35 at $157.

10. Day of the week

Wednesday is the cheapest Flash click at $1 and the cheapest Pro one at $2. Monday carries the reply: 11 of Pro's 35, at $139.

The rule

Why

A click is cheap to earn: a decent email with a link, and some people click. A reply is expensive to earn: someone has to read, believe and type. Across all 117,000 emails a positive reply was 25x rarer than a click. The stronger model earns its keep exactly where the bar is higher, and the best prompt on that model earns it again: the best Pro workflow's reply costs 37% less than its own tier's.

We have not tested the Frontier tier yet. At 5 to 10x the Pro price, the reply would have to get a lot cheaper still. We will publish that when we have it.

Method

  • Spend: everything the workflow cost before per-account discounts: lead sourcing, enrichment, sending and tokens. Not tokens alone. Each email carries its own workflow's cost per email, so a bucket's spend is the sum over the emails in it.
  • Pricing: every dollar here is what the client is charged, all in: lead sourcing, email verification, one email written for each recipient, the mailboxes and domains, sending, follow-ups, the team running it and our margin. There is no subscription and no retainer on top.
  • Website visit: the sending platform reported that the recipient clicked a link in the email. Counted once per person, on the last email sent before the click. An email with no link in the body has one anchor, the unsubscribe footer, so its clicks are unsubscribes; every visit price is measured on the emails whose body linked to the client's site. A link is a URL or a bare domain in the generated text; the sending layer turns both into a tracked link.
  • Positive reply: a recipient wrote back with interest (a question, a request for details, a meeting). Classified automatically, reviewed by a person on the account. Counted once per person, on the last email sent before the reply. Negative replies, out-of-office and unsubscribes do not count. Measured on every email, since a reply needs no link.
  • Cost per outcome: spend divided by outcomes. Every bucket with at least one outcome is drawn, and its emails and outcomes are printed under the bar so a reader can weigh it; a bucket under 1,000 emails or 20 clicks is a thin read. A cost per positive reply is stated from 10 replies, which only the best Pro workflow clears; the reply charts show the rate per 10,000 emails and the count.
  • Length, paragraphs, subject, links: measured on the generated text of the email that was sent, for each step of the sequence.
  • Reader: country, seniority, company size and industry from the lead enrichment record. Local time from the recipient's time zone where known.
  • Other studies: quoted as published, read on the date beside each. We compute nothing from them.
  • Limits: not a randomised experiment. Our optimiser assigns workflows by measured cost, so the tier, the prompt, the audience and the ask are correlated: the reply-seeking campaigns ran on Pro, in North America, without a link. Each cut is reported alone. 36 positive replies is a small count. We report counts and no confidence intervals.
