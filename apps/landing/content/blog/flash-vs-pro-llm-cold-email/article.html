The bet

Every AI vendor now sells the same model family in tiers. Flash, the cheapest. Pro, the middle. Frontier, the most expensive. Pro costs about 5x Flash per token, and everyone has a reflex about it: the pricier model must convert better, so the question is whether it converts enough better to pay for itself.

The test

We wrote 125,000 cold emails with both tiers, on real campaigns for 34 clients, and counted two things per dollar spent: how many people clicked the link, and how many wrote back with interest. Not one prompt per tier. Every client runs an A/B test across our whole catalogue of writing workflows, so the question we can answer is sharper than Flash or Pro: which workflow, on which tier, buys the outcome for the least, and by how much it beats the average.

The answer

Our best Pro workflow buys a positive reply for $139. The Pro tier as a whole pays $198; Flash pays $1,451, on too few replies to be a solid read. Our best Flash workflow buys a website visit for $1. The Flash tier as a whole pays $2, Pro $6. Each tier wins one match, and inside each tier the winning workflow beats its own average.

25 A/B tests, one winner per outcome

Each A/B test is one prompt on one model, which we call a workflow. We ran 25 of them past a thousand emails each, on the same kinds of lists. The spread between them is the whole point of running the test. The best Pro workflow buys a positive reply for $139 where its own tier pays $198, and no other workflow has enough replies to be priced yet. On the visit, 9 workflows clear the bar: the two cheapest are Flash and buy one for $1, and the cheapest Pro workflow costs $3.

This is the gap distribute.you gives its clients. Every campaign runs on the workflow with the best measured cost for what that campaign buys, across every client. A client never gets the average of the test. They get its winner, and the winner changes when the numbers do.

The twist: what the email asks for decides the outcome

The positive replies come from Pro emails with no link in them: 4.9 positive replies per 10,000 without a link against 1.6 with one. Put a link in the email and people click it instead of writing back. Leave the link out and they reply. Flash or Pro does not change that. It only changes what each one costs: on the reply, Pro is the only tier that prices one at all; on the visit, Flash is the cheaper tier by a wide margin.

The same ten cuts, Flash against Pro

We cut the click article ten ways. Here is the same knife on the tier question: for each cut, what a website visit costs on Flash and on Pro, on the emails that carried a link, and where Pro earns its positive replies. Every bucket with an outcome is drawn, with its counts under the bar. A thin bucket is a thin read and is labelled as one.

1. Which email in the sequence

The first email does the work on both tiers. Flash buys the first-email visit for $2, Pro for $3. For the reply, the first two emails are where Pro earns it: 5.1 and 4.6 positive replies per 10,000 against 2 for the second follow-up.

2. Length of the email

Short wins the visit on Flash: under 400 characters costs $1 against $2 from 500 characters up. Pro is flat across the same range and only loses the visit past 800 characters. The reply reads the other way: Pro's best reply band is 650 to 800 characters, at 11.7 per 10,000, a length Flash almost never writes.

3. Paragraphs

One paragraph buys the Flash visit for $1 and the Pro one for $3; six or more cost Pro $7. The reply wants the room: four or five paragraphs is Pro's best reply band, at 6.9 positive replies per 10,000.

4. Subject length

A subject of 30 characters or fewer buys the Flash visit for $1; past 40 characters it costs $4. Pro is flat at $3 across every band it can price. The reply does not care much either way: Pro's replies are spread evenly from 21 characters up.

5. Seniority of the reader

A director clicks a Flash email for $1 and a Pro one for $2. Founders and owners barely click at all. They reply: owners lead Pro at 7.7 positive replies per 10,000 and founders follow at 5.3, against 1.7 for a director.

6. Size of the company

Big companies click cheap: over 1,000 people, a Flash visit is $1. The one to ten person company is the dearest visit on both tiers and Pro's best reply rate, 6.2 positive replies per 10,000 against 2.5 one size band up.

7. Industry

IT and services click for $3 on Flash and $7 on Pro. Health and wellness practices do not click, they write back: 7.9 positive replies per 10,000, Pro's best industry for the reply among those past a thousand emails.

8. Where the reader is

Europe clicks cheaper than North America on both tiers: $2 against $3 on Flash, $3 against $6 on Pro. The replies are North American, because that is where the reply-seeking campaigns ran.

9. Local time of delivery

Flash clicks cheapest late, $1 between 8pm and midnight in the reader's own time zone, and dearest in the 6am to 8am rush at $4. Pro is cheapest in the small hours at $3. The reply likes the morning: 6am to 8am is the only window Pro can read solidly, at 5.8 positive replies per 10,000.

10. Day of the week

Saturday and Sunday are the cheapest Flash visits at $1; Monday is the dearest weekday on Flash and among the cheapest on Pro. Monday carries the reply, at 6.1 positive replies per 10,000, the only weekday Pro can read solidly.

The rule

Why

A visit is cheap to earn: a decent email with a link, and some people click. A reply is expensive to earn: someone has to read, believe and type. Across all 125,000 emails a positive reply was far rarer than a click. The stronger model earns its keep exactly where the bar is higher, and the best prompt on that model earns it again: the best Pro workflow's reply costs $139 against $198 for its own tier.

We have not tested the Frontier tier yet. At 5 to 10x the Pro price, the reply would have to get a lot cheaper still. We will publish that when we have it.

Method

  • Scope: sales cold email outreach only. Journalist outreach, PR pitches and every other channel we run are excluded.
  • Window: 15 April to 11 September 2026, every client account. Production data from the distribute.you platform, read on 11 September 2026.
  • Volume: 124,460 emails to 37,338 people across 34 clients, $8,412 of platform spend, 745 website visits and 36 positive replies. Of those emails, 42,325 carried a link to the client's site; they cost $2,522 and 677 people clicked. 59 workflows ran with tracked spend (a workflow is one prompt and one model), 25 of them past 1,000 emails.
  • Tiers: Flash, the cheapest model of a family (Gemini Flash, Claude Haiku, DeepSeek V4 Flash, GLM Flash, Kimi K2.6). Pro, the middle (Gemini Pro, Claude Opus, DeepSeek V4 Pro, GLM 5.3, Kimi K3). Frontier, the most expensive (Claude Fable, Astra), not yet run in production beyond a handful of emails. The model is read per email from the generation record.
  • Spend: everything the workflow cost before per-account discounts: lead sourcing, enrichment, sending and tokens. Not tokens alone. Each email carries its own workflow's cost per email, so a bucket's spend is the sum over the emails in it.
  • Pricing: every dollar here is what the client is charged, all in: lead sourcing, email verification, one email written for each recipient, the mailboxes and domains, sending, follow-ups, the team running it and our margin. There is no subscription and no retainer on top.
  • Website visit: the sending platform reported that the recipient clicked a link in the email, attributed to the email the platform named. Counted once per person, on the first click. Emails with no link in the body are excluded from every visit price, because a click needs a link to land on.
  • Link scanners: a corporate mail gateway fetches every URL in a message before the reader sees it, so a raw hit on a tracking link is not a person. On the emails we send ourselves, a hit is classified before it counts: a HEAD request, a known scanner user agent, a browser string carrying a full build number that no current browser reports, or the same reader fetching the unsubscribe link within a minute. There is no time threshold, because a person on a phone reads a message within seconds too. 271 of 309 such hits were demoted. Clicks the sending provider reports on its own infrastructure are not affected.
  • Positive reply: a recipient wrote back with interest (a question, a request for details, a meeting). Classified automatically, reviewed by a person on the account. Counted once per person, on the last email sent at or before the reply. Negative replies, out-of-office and unsubscribes do not count. Measured on every email, since a reply needs no link.
  • Cost per outcome: spend divided by outcomes. Every bucket with at least one outcome is drawn and priced, and its emails and outcomes are printed under the bar so a reader can weigh it. A cost per positive reply is stated from 10 replies, which only the best Pro workflow clears; a bucket under 1,000 emails or 20 clicks, or under 10 positive replies, is a thin read and is labelled (thin).
  • Length, paragraphs, subject, links: measured on the generated text of the email that was sent, for each step of the sequence. A link is a URL or a bare domain in that text; the sending layer turns both into a tracked link.
  • Reader: country, seniority, company size and industry from the lead enrichment record. Local time from the reader's time zone where known.
  • Other studies: quoted as published, read on the date beside each. We compute nothing from them.
  • Reproducing this: the queries and the derivation are committed at apps/landing/scripts/blog-data in our repository. Every figure on this page comes out of them.
  • Limits: not a randomised experiment. Our optimiser assigns workflows by measured cost, so the tier, the prompt, the audience and the ask are correlated: the reply-seeking campaigns ran on Pro, in North America, without a link. Each cut is reported alone. 36 positive replies is a small count. We report counts and no confidence intervals.
